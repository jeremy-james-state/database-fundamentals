Boyce–Codd normal form is a stricter third.

Every determinant must be a candidate key.

A determinant is a set of columns that determines other columns.

Third normal form mostly watches non-key columns depending on other non-key columns.

It can miss a case where a non-key determines part of the key.

That case shows up with overlapping candidate keys.

Imagine a table of rooms, times, and teachers.

A room and a time determine the teacher.

A teacher and a time determine the room.

Both pairs are candidate keys.

If you pick room plus time as the primary key, teacher is a non-key that still determines room — a piece of the key — when you also know time.

The language is dry.

The bug is a constraint you cannot say with primary key alone.

Two teachers could be assigned to the same room at the same time if you only unique the wrong pair.

BCNF says: split until every rule of the form A determines B is a key of its table.

One table for room, time, teacher with a key that matches one rule.

Another table if a second rule still needs a home.

You will join them to reconstruct the schedule.

That join is the cost of an honest constraint.

Most bookstore tables never need this conversation.

You meet it when two different unique pairs both seem right.

Do not ignore the second pair.

Put UNIQUE on it, or split until both rules are keys.

If UNIQUE on both pairs is enough for your engine to refuse bad rows, you may stop.

BCNF is the design version of that refusal.

It wants the rule to be a key, not a footnote.

When someone says a table is in third but still feels twisty, look for overlapping keys.

Name both candidate keys.

Ask whether a non-key is determining part of the primary key.

If yes, you found the leak.

Split, or add the missing unique constraint, and write down which you chose.

The classic textbook example uses something like a student, a subject, and a teacher who is the only teacher of that subject.

If each subject has one teacher, teacher determines subject.

If the key was student plus subject, teacher is a non-key determining subject, a piece of the key.

Split: one table of subject and teacher, one table of student and subject.

Now each determinant is a key of its table.

You join to see who teaches Maya.

That split is BCNF.

If the real world allows two teachers per subject, the determinant vanished and you needed a different model anyway.

The form follows the rule, not the other way around.

Write the rules in English before you chase the acronym.

A room cannot host two classes at the same time.

A teacher cannot be in two rooms at the same time.

Those are two rules.

Two rules often want two unique pairs or two tables.

BCNF is a way to notice you only enforced one.

Do not rename third normal form to Boyce–Codd on a slide to look strict.

Use it when overlapping keys appear.

Otherwise, third is the working stop.

The acronyms are a ladder.

You climb when a leak appears, not because the ladder is there.

Name the leak.

Name the determinant.

Make it a key.

That is the lesson, in one charge.

Write the rules on paper before you pick a primary key.

Each rule of the form this determines that wants to be a key somewhere.

If two rules overlap, one table may not be able to say both with a single primary key.

UNIQUE on the second pair is the small fix.

A split is the large fix.

Either fix is BCNF in spirit.

Ignoring the second rule is how double booking appears.

Double booking is not an edge case.

It is the rule you did not enforce.

Overlapping keys are rare in a bookstore.

They are common in schedules, assignments, and seats.

When you meet a schedule, remember this lesson.

Name both keys.

Enforce both.

Join if you split.

That is stricter third, in working clothes.

You do not need the acronym in production.

You need both rules on the table.

Put them there.

Then 3NF's leak is closed.

You can satisfy third normal form and still have a table that breaks.

Raymond Boyce found that in the 1970s, working examples where two candidate keys overlapped.

Codd's third form was protecting non-key columns from bad dependencies.

When a determinant was itself part of a key, third could look satisfied while a dependency still caused anomalies.

Boyce–Codd normal form says: every determinant of a functional dependency must be a candidate key.

Not merely: non-keys must not depend on non-keys.

If room determines building in a table whose keys are (student, room) and (student, building) style overlaps, you can get a mess third did not catch cleanly.

A classic shape is: a table with two overlapping candidate keys, and one attribute determining another that is also in a key.

The fix is still a split: put the determining pair in its own table whose key is the real determinant.

Boyce died young, and Codd named the form after both of them.

The story is not trivia.

It is a reminder that definitions get sharper when someone tries to break them.

Most schemas that reached a clean third never meet a Boyce–Codd violation in daily work.

When they do, it is usually overlapping natural keys you insisted on keeping.

A surrogate primary key does not automatically grant Boyce–Codd if another unique combination still determines a column badly.

Look at every uniqueness rule, not only the PRIMARY KEY line.

If A determines B, A should be a candidate key of that table.

If A is not, B does not belong here.

That one sentence is the operational form of BCNF.

Use it when third is done and a unique constraint still feels like it is smuggling a second table.

Then split, and let each table have determinants that are keys, nothing less.

You are not being fancy.

You are finishing the job third started.
