Schema design is not interior decorating.

It is a short list of goals you protect when someone asks for a new column.

The first goal is identity.

Every row should name one thing, and you should be able to find that thing again.

A primary key is how you keep that promise.

The second goal is one fact in one place.

Maya lives in Austin.

If Austin is stored on every order, a move to Denver becomes a scavenger hunt.

Store the city on the customer.

Let orders point at the customer.

The third goal is completeness.

If a fact must exist, the schema should refuse a row that lacks it.

NOT NULL is a completeness tool.

A foreign key is a completeness tool for relationships.

The fourth goal is honesty under change.

Updates should not create contradictions.

Deletes should not leave orphans unless you chose that.

The fifth goal is a schema people can query without folklore.

If you need a five-page memo to know which total is the real total, the design is leaking.

Speed is a later goal.

Indexes, copies, and caches come after the facts are in the right tables.

If you chase speed first, you freeze a messy shape.

When a new column appears in a meeting, ask which goal it serves.

If it serves none, it is probably a copy of a fact you already store.

Copies want an owner.

Either the original table, or a later denormalization you admit in public.

Protect identity first.

Then fight repetition.

Then require the facts that must exist.

Then worry about speed.

Normalization is a method for the second goal.

It is not a religion.

It is a way to notice when one table is doing three jobs.

A design that meets the goals can still be ugly on a whiteboard.

Ugly and honest beats pretty and contradictory.

Write the goals at the top of the sketch.

Identity.

One fact, one place.

Required facts required.

Change without contradiction.

Queryable without folklore.

Speed last.

Then every new column has to pass the list.

Conflicting goals will appear.

Legal retention may force you to keep a fact you would rather normalize away.

Write the conflict.

Do not hide it in a mystery column.

Audit columns — created_at, created_by — serve completeness of history, not identity of the customer.

They still belong, as long as they do not duplicate the customer.

Soft-delete flags serve honesty under change when a hard delete would smash history.

They also complicate every query with a filter.

That is a real trade, not a free goal.

International addresses will test one-fact-one-place.

A city name is not unique on Earth.

You may need a place table sooner than the first sketch thought.

That is the goal working, not failing.

When product wants a dashboard tomorrow, speed shouts.

Give them a query on honest tables first.

If it is too slow, measure, then copy with an owner.

The goal list is the agenda of that meeting.

Without it, the dashboard becomes the schema.

Teach the list to whoever can add a column.

A schema that only the original author understands has already missed the folklore goal.

Identity, one place, required facts, honest change, queryable shape, speed last.

Say it until it is boring.

Boring goals survive launches.

A goal you cannot test is a slogan.

Identity is tested by finding the same row tomorrow.

One fact in one place is tested by changing it once.

Required facts are tested by inserting a hole and watching the engine refuse.

Honest change is tested by a delete that does not orphan, or that orphans on purpose.

Queryable shape is tested by a new teammate writing a SELECT without a huddle.

Speed is tested with EXPLAIN after the rest is true.

Hang that test list next to the sketch.

Then a column request has somewhere to land besides yes.

A database that is fast and wrong is still wrong.

The five goals are not slogans.

They are tests you can fail on a Tuesday.

Consistency means two tables do not disagree about the same fact.

Integrity means the fact was allowed by the types, keys, and constraints you declared.

Together they say: one meaning, stored once, checked at the door.

If product price lives on products and also on every order line as a copy you never freeze on purpose, the confirmation page and the receipt can disagree.

That disagreement is not a rounding bug.

It is two sources of truth.

Related entities belong in related tables, sharing a key, not sharing a pile of overlapping columns.

Redundancy is extra copies of a fact that can drift.

Insert, update, and delete anomalies are what drift feels like in practice.

You change a name in one row and the old name survives in another.

You cannot insert a fact until you invent a fake parent.

You delete the last child and accidentally lose the only copy of a parent attribute.

Normalization is one way to hunt those anomalies.

Constraints are another.

The second goal is maintainability and ease of use.

A schema a new teammate can query without a huddle is easier to keep alive.

Stable naming is part of that: snake_case, table names as plurals or singulars, but not both.

customer_id everywhere, not custId in one table and customer_code in the next for the same meaning.

The third goal is performance when the facts are already true.

Indexes, types that fit, and joins on keys are how you buy speed.

Speed as a first goal produces wide tables that cannot be updated safely.

Earn integrity first, then measure the slow query.

The fourth goal is security: who may read, who may write, what is stored in the clear.

Access control and hashing belong here as design, not as a later patch.

The fifth goal is the ability to grow: a new attribute should not require rewriting history of unrelated tables.

If adding email requires copying customers into a new god table, the design was a snapshot, not a schema.

Hang the tests next to the sketch.

Identity: can I find the same row tomorrow?

One fact: can I change it once?

Required facts: does INSERT refuse a hole?

Honest change: does DELETE do what I said?

Queryable shape: can a new person write the SELECT?

A goal you cannot test is a poster on the wall.

Miss one goal and the app still ships.

The regret arrives as a receipt that does not match, or a name that changes mid-trip, or a query nobody dares to touch.

Design for the five, then optimize the one that is actually slow.

Five goals against the store: consistency so product price and receipt match unless you froze a sold price on purpose.

Maintainability so a new analyst can find orders.customer_id without folklore.

Performance so the home page has indexes after the keys are right.

Security so clerks are not admins.

Growth so a new gift-card entity is a table, not a column dump on users.

Fail one and you will meet it as a support ticket.

Test them in the order you can: integrity first, then naming, then EXPLAIN, then GRANT, then ALTER for the new entity.

That order is how the five stay a design method, not a slide title.
