An entity is a thing you can describe and tell apart from other things of the same kind.

A customer is an entity.

An order is an entity.

A book is an entity.

Austin is usually not an entity on day one.

It is a value of city, an attribute of customer.

Attributes are facts about one entity.

name, city, joined_on.

If a fact has its own facts, it wanted to be an entity.

city might grow a population, a state, a mayor.

Then city becomes a table, and customers hold city_id.

That promotion is normal.

It is not a mistake in the first sketch.

List nouns from the sentences people actually say.

A customer places an order for a book.

Three nouns.

Then list facts about each noun.

Customer: name, city, email.

Order: placed_on, total.

Book: title, year.

Check one row in your head.

Does it describe one thing?

If a row describes Maya and also Harbor Light and also a shipping box, you mixed entities.

Split until a row is one noun.

Relationships are not attributes.

customer_id on orders is not a fact about an order in the same way total is.

It is a pointer to another entity.

Keep that difference visible.

Attributes should be atomic for first normal form, which is a later lesson.

For now: one cell, one value.

Not a comma-separated pile of authors.

Derived facts are not attributes you must store.

age can be computed from birth_date.

Storing both is a chance to disagree.

Prefer the source fact.

Names are attributes; identity is a key.

Do not use name as if it were a customer.

Two Mayas exist.

Walk a new domain with sticky notes if you want.

One note per entity.

Facts on the note.

Arrows between notes.

The notes become tables.

The facts become columns.

The arrows become keys.

Events can be entities.

A login is a thing that happened.

It has a time and a customer_id.

It is not an attribute of customer unless you only store last_login_at.

last_login_at is a derived cache of the latest event.

Know which you are building.

Roles can be entities when they have facts.

admin is a flag until admins have offices and rotations.

Then role is a table.

Status values start as text and become entities when they grow labels in three languages.

Promote them when the facts appear, not before.

A good attribute name is a noun or a short noun phrase.

total, placed_at, is_active.

Avoid mystery flags named flag1.

Units belong in the name or in a separate unit column, not in the value.

total_cents versus total dollars as text with a dollar sign.

The first is an attribute.

The second is a poster.

Walk an example row with a skeptic.

If they ask whose city, you mixed entities.

If they ask which of the two titles, you mixed rows.

If they ask what the number means, you mixed attributes.

Fix the mix before you write CREATE.

The sticky notes are cheaper than ALTER.

The nouns were in the sentences all along.

Your job is to hear them.

Design does not start with tables.

It starts with messy speech: we have users, they have payment methods, orders go out, products come back as returns.

Your job is to sort subjects from characteristics, then decide which subjects are entities worth a table.

A subject is a thing you can talk about with a verb: a user has an email, an order contains lines.

A characteristic belongs to a subject: email belongs to user, not to the whole store.

Interview transcripts are full of both.

Highlight nouns that can be the subject of has, contains, places, or belongs to.

Those candidates become entities if they have more than one fact of their own, a lifecycle, and a reason to be found later.

Color might be a characteristic of product, not an entity, until you discover colors have codes, suppliers, and photos.

Then color grew up into an entity.

The test is not the English noun.

The test is whether it has attributes and identity.

Attributes become columns.

Each attribute should be one meaning: email, not contact which mixes phone and email.

If a characteristic repeats as a list in speech — a user has several payment methods — it is not a column on users.

It is a second entity related to users.

Ignore noise: adverbs, one-off anecdotes, and attributes nobody will query or constrain.

You can store a note, but you should not invent a table for a sentence said once.

Name entities in the language of the business, then spell them in snake_case when they become tables.

user is an entity.

users is a table if you chose plural tables.

Do not let the sketch and the SQL drift in names.

After you have entities and attributes, you still do not have keys.

Keys are the next lesson.

Here the win is a clean inventory: these are the things, these are the facts about each thing, these lists of facts are actually more things.

If two subjects share a pile of attributes, ask whether they are the same entity in costume.

Customer and user might be one table or two, depending on whether a user can exist without ever buying.

That question is a requirement, not a taste in naming.

Write the entities on boxes.

Write attributes inside.

Leave the lines for the relationship lesson.

A box with twenty attributes is a warning that several subjects are hiding in one list.

Split the box before you draw the first key.

Parse one interview sentence: a user has an account, payment methods, and a cart of products.

Subjects: user, account, payment method, cart, product.

Characteristics: whatever facts they mentioned — email on user, last four on payment method, quantity in the cart.

Account might be the same as user if login is one-to-one with the person.

Ask.

Cart might be a table of lines, not a column of product names.

Payment methods are many, so not columns card1 card2 on users.

Product is its own entity because it has name, price, manufacturer.

Manufacturer might be text on product until you have manufacturer addresses and contacts.

Then it graduates.

Throw away the word quickly from the interview; it is not an attribute.

Keep email, because uniqueness and login will need it.

This noun work is the whole conversion from real-world ideas to tables.

Tables come after the inventory is honest.
