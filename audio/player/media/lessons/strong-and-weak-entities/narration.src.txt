Some things in the world carry their own identity.

A customer is one of those.

You can talk about Maya without mentioning an order.

Her customer_id is enough.

That is a strong entity.

The table has a primary key that does not need another table to make sense.

A weak entity cannot stand on that kind of key alone.

Think of an order line: line one, two copies of Harbor Light.

Line one on which order?

Every order has a line one.

The line number only identifies a row when you also know the order.

The identity is borrowed.

The parent order is the owner.

The child's key is usually the owner's key plus a discriminator.

order_id and line_no together.

That composite key is a feature, not clutter.

If you hide the parent, the child row stops making sense.

Ask that question out loud.

Does this row still name one thing if I erase the parent?

If the answer is no, you have a weak entity.

Rooms in a building work the same way.

Room 12 in which building?

Seats on a flight.

Seat 14A on which flight number?

Do not invent a random line_id just to pretend the child is strong.

You can, and applications sometimes do.

Then you still need a unique pair of order_id and line_no, or you will insert two line ones.

The surrogate hides the weakness.

It does not remove it.

On a diagram, a weak entity is often a double box.

The relationship to the owner is often a double diamond.

You do not need the symbols to use the idea.

You need the question about identity.

Foreign keys and weak entities overlap but are not the same.

orders.customer_id is a foreign key on a strong entity.

The order still has order_id of its own.

A weak child uses the parent's key as part of its own primary key.

That is a tighter bond.

Deleting the owner without the children is almost always wrong.

The children were never independent facts.

When you model, list the nouns.

Circle the ones that already have a passport.

Underline the ones that only make sense as a part of something else.

Give the parts a composite key.

Give the passports a simple key.

Then the schema tells you which rows can travel alone.

Ownership is not the same as a mere pointer.

An order points at a customer and still has its own life.

An order line is owned.

You would not archive a line in a warehouse without the order header.

That operational fact is the model.

When teams argue about line_id, ask how they pick up the phone.

Do they say line forty-two globally, or line two on order one-oh-one?

The phone script is the key.

History tables sometimes freeze a weak entity into a strong one by giving it a surrogate and copying the parent id as a plain foreign key.

That can be right for analytics.

It is a different job than checkout.

Do not let the warehouse rewrite the checkout key without a meeting.

Identifying relationships are the ER phrase for the weak bond.

Non-identifying relationships are foreign keys that are not part of the child's primary key.

Use the phrases if your diagram tool does.

Use the identity test if it does not.

Cascade deletes follow ownership.

They do not follow every arrow.

A weak child almost always cascades.

A strong child almost always restricts.

Write that next to the box.

Examples besides lines: chapters of a book if you identify them as chapter three of book eleven.

Comments on a review if they are numbered only inside the review.

If comments have public permalink ids, they became strong.

The world gets to decide.

Your schema records the decision.

Revisit it when permalinks appear.

Until then, composite keys keep the weakness visible, which is a kindness to the next designer.

Strong and weak are not about how important a table feels.

They are about whether a thing can be identified on its own.

Cardinality is how many: one, zero, many.

Weakness is how identity works.

Do not mix those two dials.

A strong entity has its own unique identifier.

A purchase with purchase_id is strong even if it points at a customer.

Depending on another table for a foreign key is normal.

Depending on another table to exist as an identity is the weak case.

A weak entity cannot identify a row with only its own attributes.

Its key is usually composite: the parent's key plus something local.

A line on an order might be identified by order_id plus line_number.

Line number three means nothing until you know which order.

That is a weak entity, or at least a dependent identity.

Beginners mark everything that 'belongs to' something as weak.

An employee has a manager, so employee must be weak.

No.

Employee still has employee_id.

Belonging is a relationship.

Identity is a key.

Use a weak design when the child is meaningless and unidentifiable without the parent, and you will never look it up by a surrogate of its own.

Room 12 in building A is not the same as room 12 in building B.

If the business says room numbers restart per building, the room is identified by building plus number.

If every room gets a global room_id, you made it strong on purpose.

That is allowed.

You are choosing an identifier, not discovering a law of nature.

When you do use a weak entity, the relationship to the parent is identifying.

The parent's key is part of the child's key.

Deleting the parent without a plan orphans identity itself, not just a pointer.

In an ER diagram, weak entities are often shown with a double rectangle, and the identifying relationship with a double diamond, in Chen style.

Crow's foot sketches may instead show the composite key in the table box.

Pick a notation and stay consistent.

The rare honest case is a child whose natural key is not unique until the parent is included.

The common dishonest case is a regular table you were afraid to give an id.

Give it an id if it is a thing you will query, update, and relate on its own.

Keep it weak if its name is always 'the third line of that order' and never a thing with a passport of its own.

Ask: can I find this row tomorrow without naming the parent?

If yes, it is strong.

If no, either add a surrogate and make it strong, or accept the composite and call it weak on purpose.

Purpose is the whole lesson.

Weak is a design choice with a cost.

Do not spend it on ordinary belongs-to relationships.

Walk a receipt.

The purchase has purchase_id, a strong entity.

Each line is line_number one, two, three inside that purchase.

Line number two in another purchase is a different thing.

Identity of a line is purchase_id plus line_number.

That is weak, or identifying dependent, on purpose.

Do not give the line a global line_id if the business will only ever say the third line of order 5001.

Do give a global id if warehouse software will move lines between orders and still talk about line 88901.

That second world made the line strong.

A dependent person on an employee benefits form is the textbook weak example: name plus employee_id, because dependents are not issued employee numbers.

If your company later issues every dependent a person_id, they stopped being weak.

The ER mark changed because the identifiers changed, not because cardinality changed.

One employee still has many dependents.

That was always one-to-many.

Weakness was only about the key.

When not to use weak: any table you will load, search, and relate without reciting the parent id every time.

Users, products, invoices with invoice_id, shipments with tracking numbers.

Those are strong.

Mislabeling them weak clutters the diagram with double lines that mean nothing in SQL.

SQL still wants a PRIMARY KEY either way.

The weak case just makes that key composite and identifying.

Choose with the find-this-tomorrow test, then draw.
